# The 'Buy Now, Pay Later' Debt Trap: What Is It and Why Is It Dangerous?

## The Attractiveness of "Buy Now, Pay Later" Services
Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) has emerged as a mainstream financial practice in just a few years. By 2025, the value of BNPL transactions in the United States is estimated to reach $70 billion, and BNPL companies such as Klarna, Afterpay, and Affirm are already integrated into the checkout process of many e-commerce websites. It's not just the wealthiest segment of the population that uses it, either: Bankrate survey data on U.S. consumers reveals that the $50K to $79K income bracket is the highest user group, with 36% using it, while about 31% of both the sub-$50K and over-$100K income brackets use it as well. It's not only for those who can't afford to pay in cash; it's become a way of paying for a large swath of shoppers.
 
The appeal is genuine: no lengthy credit check, instant approval, and no single payment that seems too much to handle. But convenience and safety aren't the same thing, and the evidence of what this means in the real-life experiences of consumers is more complex than the marketing would suggest.
 
## How BNPL Works: Convenience at a Cost  
The principle behind BNPL is dividing a purchase into smaller chunks, usually four payments over six weeks. The most widely used model is "pay-in-four." Data from six major providers shows that the average BNPL loan amount was $135, while the average user had 6.3 loans per provider per year, indicating that most users aren't taking out one BNPL loan and then forgetting about it.
 
What counts is that self-reported late payments have been on the rise. Nearly half of BNPL users (47%) paid late at least once over the last 12 months, up from 41% in 2025 and 34% in 2024. That's a fairly large figure for what's marketed as an easy, low-risk product.
 
## Understanding the Psychological Factors Influencing BNPL Adoption
The reason BNPL is so popular isn't a coincidence, it's based on a well-documented behavioural phenomenon known as the "pain of payment." Paying in smaller chunks genuinely makes spending feel easier, even when the total price is identical to paying it all at once. Interestingly, LendingTree's 2026 survey found that being interest-free was the least popular reason for choosing BNPL, with just 11% citing it as their primary reason. Ease of use (31%) and ease of approval (27%) were the top two reasons. In other words, it's not about the money, it's about feeling better, without necessarily being better for you.

## Hidden Fees and Accumulating Debt: The Dark Side  
The bright side, according to CFPB data, is that the average late fee has been declining at the industry level: the average late fee for 2023 was $9.99, with only 4.1% of loans being charged a late fee that year, down from 5.2% in 2022. Even if you do get charged, there's a good chance it'll be reduced or waived. A mid-2026 survey found that 90% of people who requested a late fee waiver saw their fee cut or waived entirely. The "hidden fees will destroy you" rhetoric often used in BNPL warnings isn't quite as strong at the individual-loan level.
 
The real risk is structural, not fee-based: loan stacking. Recent research indicates that 63% of BNPL users have more than one loan in place at the same time, and 33% are using more than one BNPL provider simultaneously. Unlike a credit card statement, which shows your total balance, there's no single place to view your total BNPL exposure, since each provider keeps its own records. LendingTree also found a significant rise in the number of people using BNPL for everyday items such as groceries, reflecting cash-flow pressure rather than discretionary spending on wants.
 
## The Impact on Financial Health and Credit Scores
This is changing quickly, and most consumers aren't yet aware. In the past, the traditional credit system was largely blind to BNPL loans: if a payment was missed, it might go to collections, but the loan itself typically wouldn't appear on a credit report. That's ending. In 2025, Affirm began reporting loan data to credit bureaus. Other providers are feeling pressure to follow suit, and in fall 2025, FICO released a new scoring model called Score 10 BNPL, specifically designed to incorporate BNPL payment history. For years, it was assumed BNPL wouldn't affect your credit score. That's no longer a safe assumption.
 
Another clear overlap between BNPL use and financial fragility: approximately 60% of U.S. BNPL borrowers are classified as subprime or near-subprime, and the average BNPL user's credit card utilization rate sits between 60 and 66%, compared with 34% for non-BNPL users. That doesn't mean BNPL creates financial strain, it means the people most likely to use BNPL are often already facing financial strain for other reasons.

## Regulatory Gaps
BNPL simply doesn't carry the same consumer protections as credit cards. In 2024, the CFPB proposed a rule to apply credit-card-like protections (such as standardized dispute rights) to BNPL loans, but in May 2025 the agency rescinded the rule and announced it would not publish a replacement. That means BNPL remains less strictly regulated than conventional credit in the U.S., and disclosure and consumer protections vary widely between providers. According to the Richmond Fed, the fragmented, hard-to-track debt load is an emerging systemic-risk concern, one caused not by any single loan but by the inability of regulators, or consumers, to see the full picture.

## Smarter Alternatives to Avoid the Debt Trap  
If you want flexibility without the stacking risk, here are a few concrete alternatives worth considering instead of defaulting to BNPL at every purchase:
 
- **A 0% intro APR credit card**, assuming you actually plan to pay it off during the promotional period. It offers the same "spread out the cost" benefit, but with a single, easy-to-track balance.
- **A brief delay before non-essential purchases.** Even 24 to 48 hours makes impulsive buying less likely, since BNPL's whole appeal rests on removing the pause before you buy.
- **A simple running list of all your active BNPL loans.** Providers don't share this data with each other, so the only way to track your total exposure is to keep your own up-to-date record.
- **A budgeting app, or, if your debt load is larger, a nonprofit credit counselor.** Both are especially useful once BNPL, or any kind of recurring debt, starts becoming hard to keep track of.

## The Bottom Line
There's nothing inherently deceptive or manipulative about BNPL. The fee and default data actually looks better than the worst headlines suggest. The real danger lies in its ease of use, the psychological trick that makes it feel less painful than spending normally does, its invisibility to any single lender monitoring your exposure, and, as of 2025 to 2026, its growing effect on your credit score. Used sparingly and monitored closely, it's a reasonably fair payment method. But it's also the kind of debt that's easy to forget about until it's too late, one that several providers let you take on by default without ever showing you the total.
